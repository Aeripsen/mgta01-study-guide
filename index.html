The exam is two sections of 20 marks. One is multiple choice. One is short answer, bullet points only, built around applying the course to case studies.

The thing worth knowing before you start

Only about half of this is memorisation. The MC section rewards knowing definitions cold. The short answer section is explicitly about application to cases, and the marks there go to naming the right concept, applying it to the specific business in front of you, and reaching a defensible conclusion. Reciting a definition into a case question scores roughly half of what it could.

So: memorise the definitions, then practise pointing them at a company.

The course has one spine, repeated in almost every lecture. Hold it and most of the material hangs off it:

satisfy customers → to make a profit → to keep satisfying customers

And one equation, which is the definition of a business rather than an accounting detail:

PROFIT = REVENUE − EXPENSES   (negative result = LOSS)

The ten units below are not ten topics. They are one argument in order: what a business is, what it sells, what it is made of, who owns those parts, how it competes, how we measure the whole system, who starts one, how they plan it, what legal shape it takes, and how they pay for it.

01Business

What a business actually is

An organisation is any group working toward a shared goal. A business is the subset of organisations that intends to make a profit. The intent is the whole distinction.

Organization
A group of people working together to meet a need or pursue common goals. A family, a soccer team, a campus club.
Business
An organised effort to provide things people need and want and will pay for, in order to satisfy those needs and make a profit.
Profit motive
The idea that people give up time, energy and money to start a business only if they can realise some benefit from doing so.
Not-for-profit
Provides things people need, may collect revenue, but is not intended to make a profit. A surplus is retained to run the organisation, not paid to an owner.
Revenue
Money flowing in each time a product or service is sold.
Expenses
The costs incurred in the effort to generate that revenue.
Memory hook · the six characteristics

Do not memorise six bullets. Memorise one sentence that runs in order, because the six characteristics are a sequence, not a list:

You organise effort to provide what people need, you must satisfy customers, which earns revenue, against which you pay expenses, and what is left is profit.

The argument about whether profit is good

Two named thinkers, and the exam expects you to be able to attribute the position, not just describe it.

Karl Marx · profit is exploitation

Owners (capitalists) grow wealthy by exploiting the labour of workers (labourers). If a worker is paid $10 an hour and the owner clears $40 from what they made, the gap is extracted value. Leads to communism: the state should own business activity so no one grows rich off another's labour.

Adam Smith · profit is fair reward for risk

Rational people only commit time, energy and money where there is reward for the risk. The surplus is not greed, it buys equipment, maintenance and marketing, and it creates the jobs in the first place. Leads to liberalism: leave people to their self-interest with minimum government interference, and society gains most.

The risk is real, and the numbers are the point. Canada is among the easiest places on earth to start a business, and Canadian entrepreneurs create roughly 140,000 businesses a year. In the same period roughly 120,000 close. Amazon lost around $3 billion over eight years before its first profit. Easy to start, very hard to keep.

02Products

What a business sells

A product is whatever the buyer believes they are getting. It splits into goods and services, and every difference between them traces back to one fact: a good is a thing, a service is an event.

The value package

FUNCTION + FEATURES + BENEFITS = VALUE

Function is what it is meant to do. A feature is an extra attribute improving usefulness. A benefit is the advantage the buyer derives. This matters because raising revenue means raising price or volume, and both require raising value, real or perceived.

Three kinds of consumer product

ConvenienceCheap, frequent, no thought. Milk, gum.
ShoppingModerately expensive, infrequent, comparison shopping. Appliances.
SpecialtyHigh importance, significant time and effort to find exactly the right one. A wedding dress.
Less effort per purchaseMore effort

Separately, B2C products go to the end user, B2B (industrial) products are the parts, materials and supplies one business buys from another. Plenty of things are both.

Memory hook · the cycle I G M D

Introduction, Growth, Maturity, Decline. Two numbers worth carrying: 80% of new products fail in the introduction stage, and saturation is the word for a market that can absorb no more. An extension is any effort to repackage, relaunch or update a mature but well-known product to stretch the curve.

03Factors

What a business is made of

Four building blocks combine to create any business. Three of them are inputs. The fourth, entrepreneurship, is the thing that assembles the other three.

Natural resources
Things found in nature, grown from or extracted from the earth. Land, water, wood, oil, wheat.
Labour
The people who contribute their efforts. No business exists without at least one worker.
Capital
Money, or the machines and technologies money can buy.
Entrepreneurship
The willingness to take initiative and accept the risk of failure in return for suitable reward.
Factor substitution
Replacing one factor with another so products can be made faster or cheaper. Self-checkouts replacing cashiers, ATMs replacing tellers.
Factor intensity
All businesses need all factors, but one usually dominates. Farming is resource intensive, Walmart labour intensive, auto parts capital intensive, Meta information intensive.

A business is a cell built from four parts. If the course adds information as a proposed fifth factor, remember it is presented as a theory, not settled fact.

04Economies

Who owns the factors

An economic system answers four questions: who owns the factors, who controls them, who decides what gets produced, and who decides how it is distributed. The answer runs on a single axis from total state control to total private control.

Planned · the case for

Only the state has the power to feed, clothe, shelter, educate and employ a population. Businesses only care about profit, the state has a benevolent interest in everyone's welfare. The weakness: central planners work from models, models do not guarantee the right quantities, and bureaucracies are slow to adapt.

Market · the case for

People's needs are too vast for any one entity to plan. Officials are too removed from ordinary needs and lack the information and motivation. Competition keeps enterprise efficient. Power concentrated in few hands tends to corrupt. The weakness: large private firms get bureaucratic too.

Canada is a mixed market economy. The state does three things.

  • Imposes and collects taxes. Personal income tax, which is progressive, meaning a larger percentage from higher earners. Corporate income tax. Sales tax, 13% HST in Ontario. Employment insurance, about 2%.
  • Passes laws regulating business. Canadian Human Rights Act, Canada Labour Code, Employment Insurance Act, Competition Act (bans misleading advertising), Consumer Packaging and Labelling Act.
  • Provides some goods and services through crown corporations: CBC, Canada Post, LCBO, Hydro One, OLG.
Memory hook · historical terms

Four countries, four words for the same move toward state control, then two words for the move back:

RU
Nationalization — state assumes ownership
US
New Deal — 1930s laws to manage the economy
UK
State-owned enterprise — provides goods, seeks no profit
CN
Great Leap Forward — Mao abolished private land ownership

Going back the other way: perestroika (the 1980s Soviet reversal) and privatization (transferring ownership out of government hands). A command economy is a planned economy with more authoritarianism implied.

05Markets

How markets compete

A market is not a place, it is the interaction of buyers and sellers. The single variable that defines a market structure is how many sellers there are, because that sets both buyer choice and seller pricing power.

Market
The interactions of buyers and sellers exchanging information about products for sale. An abstract idea, not a physical place.
Target market
A group sharing similarities (age, gender, income, ethnicity, location) with similar needs, identified by the seller as most likely to buy.
Market share
One firm's sales as a percentage of total sales in that market.
Barriers to entry
What makes an industry difficult, slow or expensive to enter. Technology, scale, capital intensity, government regulation.
Barriers to exit
What makes it difficult to leave. Contracts, large investment, specialised equipment.
Differentiation
Convincing customers your product is different or better than competitors'.
Branding
Getting customers to recognise name, logo, colours and slogan, and so differentiate you.
Memory hook · two kinds of monopoly

Natural monopoly wins by economics: size, efficiency or exclusive access makes it permanently cheaper than any rival. Legislated monopoly wins by law: the government granted the exclusive right. LCBO is legislated. The distinction is a clean multiple choice question, so learn which word means which.

06Measuring

Measuring the whole economy

Six measures, and each one exists because the one before it was incomplete. Learn them as a chain of complaints and you cannot mix them up.

MeasureWhat it capturesWhy the next one exists
GDPSizeTotal value of goods and services produced in a country in a year. But size alone says nothing about direction.
GDP growthProgressPercentage change period to period. But a growing economy with a faster growing population is getting poorer per person.
GDP per capitaProsperityGDP divided by population, the wealth of the average citizen. But it does not say what caused it.
ProductivityThe cause of prosperityOutput relative to inputs used. More output from less input is more productive.
EmploymentUse of labourWhether the system is using its people. But the average can hide the distribution.
DistributionEqualityLorenz curve and Gini coefficient. A high average can hide a few very rich and many poor.
The instructor said this outright

You will not have to calculate a Gini coefficient. You do need to understand its relative meaning: 0 is perfect equality, 1 is extreme inequality, and therefore closer to 0 is more equal. Learn the direction, skip the arithmetic.

Recession
Two consecutive quarters of declining GDP. A natural part of the business cycle.
Business cycle
The expansion and contraction of national economic activity over years, with regularity. Booms and busts.
Depression
An unusually long or deep recession, such as the United States 1929–1932.
Labour force
People 15 and over who have a job or business, plus those without work who are actively seeking it.
Unemployment rate
The percentage of the labour force actively seeking work but unable to find it.
Under-employment
Working, but not at full potential. Not counted in unemployment.
Discouraged workers
People who gave up looking, judging their chances too remote. Also not counted.
Working poor
Employed, but on incomes too low to save or afford necessities.
Memory hook · the trap in the unemployment rate

The rate counts only people actively seeking work. So both the under-employed and discouraged workers are invisible in it, and the headline number therefore understates the problem. If a case question asks why unemployment looks fine but the economy does not, that is the answer.

07Entrepreneurs

Who starts businesses

Entrepreneurship is a temperament, not a job title. Three psychological traits, one theory of motivation, one theory of control, and three kinds of inheritance.

Memory hook · the three traits ART
A
Achievement — a need to achieve challenging goals
R
Risk-taking propensity — belief in one's ability to influence affairs
T
Tolerance of ambiguity — willingness to sit with uncertainty

Entrepreneurship is an art. The three traits reinforce each other: high achievement need correlates with internal locus of control, which correlates with higher risk-taking.

McClelland's theory of needs

We are each driven by a mix of three motivational needs, which shape personality, behaviour and career choice.

nPow · Power

Motivated by authority and influence. Chooses careers with status and prestige: senior managers, military officers, politicians.

nAff · Affiliation

Motivated by friendly relationships and interaction. Teachers, doctors and nurses, religious leaders, coaches, team roles.

nAch · Achievement

Motivated by personal accomplishment, sets and attains challenging goals. Athletes, scholars, entrepreneurs.

For achievement-motivated people specifically: the sense of achievement matters more than financial reward, accomplishment gives more satisfaction than praise, money is a measurement of success rather than the goal, and neither security nor status is a prime motivator.

Locus of control

InternalOutcomes are shaped by my own behaviour, actions and effort.
ExternalOutcomes are the result of fate, luck or outside forces I cannot control.
Entrepreneurs cluster here

Rotter's theory, and everyone sits somewhere on the continuum. Internal locus means taking responsibility for successes and failures, learning from them, and trying again.

A distinction the exam can test in one word

Risk tolerance is not risk seeking. Risk tolerance treats new situations as exciting and possibly desirable. Risk seeking is carelessness about consequences. Entrepreneurs are not gamblers, they take calculated risks: undertaken after weighing advantages and disadvantages and estimating the probability of outcomes. Risk aversion is the opposite end, treating the new as threatening.

Memory hook · the three inheritances EVE
E
Entrepreneurial — characteristics and values absorbed from an entrepreneurial parent
V
Vocational — job-related skills observed and learned from parents
E
Economic — financial capital received to support starting a business

About 80% of Canadian small business owners come from a family business background.

Two demographic findings worth quoting

  • Immigrants make strong entrepreneurs, and are both pulled (their characteristics of self-confidence, reasonable risk-taking and high achievement need match the entrepreneurial profile) and pushed (lacking local networks, Canadian experience and track record, so enterprise becomes the available option). Most are economic migrants, moving to improve their economic wellbeing.
  • Entrepreneurs skew older. More than half of Canadian businesses are owned by people over 50, and the highest rate of new business creation belongs to the 55–64 group. Four reasons, all starting with C: capital, contacts, credibility, confidence.
Sizes, since these are testable numbers

Small is under 50 employees for services or under 100 for goods. Medium is 100–499. Large is 500 or more. SME covers small and medium together. There is no single official definition, and other thresholds used include under $5M revenue, under $1M exports, under $500K profits.

08Planning

Planning the business

A business plan is the written document laying out everything that has to happen for the planned business to succeed. Writing one makes an entrepreneur six times more likely to actually start.

The owner's checklist, in order

#StepWhat it means
1Choose a nicheWhat will the business do, and who will it target? Assess your own experience, strengths and skills, then assess the target market and competitors.
2Write the business planThink like a business, not just a service provider.
3Register as a legal entitySole proprietorship, partnership or corporation. See unit 9.
4Fulfil other legal requirementsInsurance, business bank account, bookkeeping. Agreements: business, independent contractor, NDA, non-compete, proposal, invoice.
5Build a marketing planExisting network first, then industry engagement and networking, then be easy to find. Advertising last.
6Deliver great resultsKeep upskilling, ask for feedback often, optimise. Testimonials matter.

The nine sections of a business plan

Grouped into three concerns, which is far easier to recall than nine separate headings.

Framing

Executive summary. Mission, vision and strategic objectives. Business and industry overview.

The market

Market analysis and competition. Sales and marketing plan.

The people

Ownership and management plan. Your credentials matter a great deal here.

The resources

Operating plan. Financial plan. Appendices and exhibits.

Memory hook · why the plan exists

Two reasons, and a case question will want both. It proves the entrepreneur is serious, and it proves they have done the research and the work. That is why it is required for a pitch to investors or a bank loan, not just for the founder's own benefit.

09Structures

The three legal structures

Everything about this unit turns on one axis: liability. Simple structures are easy and cheap and leave you personally exposed. The corporation is complex and expensive and puts a legal wall between you and the business.

 Sole proprietorshipPartnershipCorporation
Speed and cost of setupLowLowHigh
Personal liabilityHighHighLow
Tax breaksLowLowHigh
Loan and investment accessLowLowHigh
Annual paperworkLowLowHigh
Ease of ownership transferLowLowHigh
Level of controlHighMediumLow
Ease of dissolutionHighMediumLow
Memory hook · read the table as one trade

You do not need to memorise 24 cells. Memorise the trade: as you move right, you give up control and simplicity to buy protection and access to money. Sole proprietorship is all control and all risk. Corporation is all protection and all paperwork. Partnership sits in the middle on control and dissolution, and stays with the sole proprietor on liability.

The two exceptions to the pattern, which is where MC questions live: annual paperwork and setup cost run the opposite way to the other benefits, because there the corporation is the worse option.

Sole proprietorship
Directly owned by one person. Most common form of ownership. One owner, but can have many employees. Supplies all capital, makes all decisions, keeps all profit, pays all expenses and taxes, responsible for all debts.
Liability
The legal and financial responsibility for something.
Personal liability
The owner's personal responsibility to pay the business's costs and settle its obligations.
Unlimited liability
No cap on what the owner might pay. Not limited by the size of their investment in the business.
General partnership
Directly owned by two or more individuals who agree to act together.
Joint and several liability
An obligation made by one partner is binding on all the others. Know and trust your partners.
Partnership agreement
Document spelling out obligations: capital contributed, decision and voting power, roles, pay, profit division, dispute resolution.
Corporation
A legal entity created by an act of law to own and operate a business. From the Latin corpus, body.
Limited liability
An individual's financial responsibility is limited to the capital they provided. Shareholders have limited liability, the corporation itself has unlimited liability.
Share
An ownership stake, entitling the owner to a share of profits. Acquired by investing capital or as compensation for work.

A corporation is an artificial person

That is not a metaphor, it is the legal mechanism. It has the same rights and duties as a person: to borrow and lend, buy and sell property, hire and fire, pay taxes, sue and be sued. It also exists permanently. The Japanese construction firm Kongō Gumi, founded in 578 CE, is the oldest company in the world.

Creating one takes five steps: choose a name, conduct a name search, complete the articles of incorporation, submit the paperwork and fee, then receive the certificate of incorporation. Corporations must legally carry a word or abbreviation after the name (Corp, Inc, Ltd, Co) to signal limited liability. Private corporations are owned by a small closely connected group and do not offer shares publicly. Public corporations sell shares to the general public, and Canada has about 4,200 of them.

10Financing

Paying for it

Four sources of capital. The way to remember them is by what each one costs you, because no source is free and the price is different every time.

Memory hook · four sources, four prices
1
Founder equity — your own money. Costs you your savings.
2
Grants — free money. Costs you paperwork and a perfect fit.
3
Debt — borrowed money. Costs you interest, and it must be repaid on time.
4
Investor equity — sold money. Costs you control.

Ordered by how much of the company you keep. Grants are the only source that costs neither ownership nor repayment, which is exactly why they are scarce and competitive.

 Equity capitalDebt capital
Money inGivenLent
Money outNever repaidMust be repaid
Voting rightsInvestors get votesLenders get none
OwnershipInvestors get ownershipLenders get none
Return to providerA share of profitInterest
Finance
All the activities involved in locating, collecting, organising and distributing capital.
Equity capital
Money put into a business in exchange for a share of ownership, decision-making and profits.
Debt capital
Money lent to a business. Must be repaid, usually with interest.
Interest
The fee paid to the lender for the right to use the money until it is repaid.
Dilution
A decrease in the proportion owned by existing shareholders after new investors put equity in.
Capital structure
The combination of equity and debt a business chooses to finance operations and growth.
Investor
Provides capital expecting the enterprise to grow, profit, and return more capital later.
Grant
A sum awarded, often by government or a non-profit, to support specific activities. Not repaid, does not dilute ownership.

Investor equity comes in a rough order as the business grows: friends and family first, then angel investors, then venture capital. Expect to raise more than once.

The trade-off the exam will ask you to argue

Equity gives you capital plus the investor's skills and expertise, and costs you equity, decision-making power and ultimately control. Debt gives you capital with no dilution at all, and costs you repayment with interest on a schedule, more risk if you cannot pay, and it is harder for young owners to access. One buys help at the price of control, the other keeps control at the price of obligation.

Drill

Ten multiple choice in the style of the paper, then the method for the short answer half. Answers are hidden until you click. Do not click early.

Multiple choice

1. A campus club charges membership fees and pays a part-time coordinator. It is not a business because it
  • collects no revenue
  • has no employees
  • does not exist for the purpose of making a profit
  • is not registered with the government

C. Organisations can charge for things and can pay people. The profit motive is the only line between a business and another kind of organisation. A and B are factually wrong about this club, and D is irrelevant.

2. An airline cannot sell an empty seat after the plane departs. This illustrates
  • variability
  • perishability
  • intangibility
  • inseparability

B. Perishability is the inability to store the product for later sale. Variability would be no two flights being identical, intangibility that you cannot touch the service, inseparability that it is produced and consumed at once. All four are true of the airline, which is why you must read what the stem actually describes.

3. A product's sales are still climbing, marketing spend has fallen, and profit is at its highest point ever. Which stage?
  • Introduction
  • Growth
  • Maturity
  • Decline

C. The giveaway is profit at its highest combined with lower marketing spend. Growth is where a product first turns a profit, maturity is where profit peaks because awareness is already high so you stop paying to build it.

4. A grocery chain replaces ten cashiers with self-checkout machines. This is best described as
  • economies of scale
  • factor substitution
  • factor intensity
  • the information revolution

B. Substituting capital for labour so products can be provided more cheaply. Factor intensity would be describing which factor dominates the business overall. Economies of scale is cost falling as volume rises, a different mechanism.

5. In Canada, the LCBO's exclusive position is an example of
  • a natural monopoly
  • a legislated monopoly
  • an oligopoly
  • monopolistic competition

B. The exclusivity was granted by government legislation, not earned through size or efficiency. A natural monopoly is one that would always be cheaper than rivals for reasons of scale, efficiency or exclusive access.

6. A country's GDP rose 3% while its population rose 5%. The most accurate statement is
  • prosperity increased because GDP grew
  • GDP per capita fell, so the average citizen is worse off
  • the country entered a recession
  • the Gini coefficient must have risen

B. This is precisely why GDP per capita exists as a separate measure from GDP growth. C is wrong because a recession requires two consecutive quarters of declining GDP, and GDP grew. D is unrelated, distribution is a different question from average.

7. Two partners run a design firm. One signs an expensive lease without telling the other. The other partner is
  • not liable, since they did not sign
  • liable only up to their investment
  • fully liable, because of joint and several liability
  • liable only if a partnership agreement exists

C. An obligation made by one partner binds all of them, and liability is unlimited so it is not capped at the investment. B describes limited liability, which is a feature of corporations. A partnership agreement governs the partners' dealings with each other, it does not protect them from a third party.

8. Shareholders of a corporation
  • appoint the officers directly
  • elect the directors, who appoint the officers
  • must also work for the corporation
  • have unlimited liability for its debts

B. Learn the two verbs: shareholders elect, directors appoint. C is wrong because ownership and work are separated, which is the whole point of the structure. D reverses the rule, shareholders have limited liability.

9. A founder takes $500,000 from an angel investor for 25% of the company. The most significant cost to the founder is
  • interest payments
  • the repayment schedule
  • dilution of ownership and loss of decision-making control
  • the loss of limited liability

C. A and B are the costs of debt, not equity, and equity is never repaid. Equity's price is control. D is irrelevant, taking investment does not change the liability structure.

10. Country X has a Gini coefficient of 0.25 and country Y has 0.55. Which is true?
  • X is wealthier than Y
  • income is more equally distributed in X than in Y
  • X has higher GDP per capita
  • Y has lower unemployment

B. Closer to 0 means more equal. Note carefully that the Gini says nothing about how wealthy a country is, only how evenly what it has is spread. A and C confuse distribution with prosperity, which is exactly the trap.

The short answer half, which is worth the same 20 marks

The method

Bullet points only, and the question will be built on a case. Every answer should do three things, in this order, because that is where the marks sit:

  1. Name the concept. Use the course's exact term. "This is monopolistic competition." One bullet.
  2. Define it in a clause. Enough to prove you know it, not a paragraph.
  3. Apply it to the actual company in the case, quoting the specific details given. This is the part people skip, and it is usually worth more than the other two combined.

The single most common failure is writing a correct general essay about a concept that never mentions the business in the scenario. If the case names a coffee shop with three competitors and a price increase, your answer has to talk about that coffee shop, those competitors and that price.

Worked example — a case in the style of the paper, with a model answer

Maya runs a small bakery in a neighbourhood with four other bakeries. Hers is the only one with a recognisable logo, a name people repeat, and a signature sourdough people travel for. She charges 20% more than the others and still sells out. She now wants to open three more locations and needs $400,000. She has $60,000 saved and no assets to secure a loan against.

a) Identify the market structure and justify it.

  • Monopolistic competition.
  • Many sellers with low barriers to entry, but a small number differentiate through branding and can charge more.
  • Applies here: five bakeries in one neighbourhood is many sellers with low barriers, but Maya has a recognisable logo and a signature product, which is differentiation and branding.
  • The evidence is that she charges 20% more and still sells out, which is pricing power that perfect competition would not allow.

b) Recommend a financing route and state the trade-off.

  • She has $60,000 of founder equity, leaving $340,000 to raise.
  • Debt is difficult: no assets to secure against, and the course notes younger owners have less access to debt capital.
  • So investor equity, likely angel investors given the amount and stage.
  • The trade-off: she gets the capital plus the investor's skills and expertise, but accepts dilution and loss of decision-making power, so less control over the brand that is currently the source of her pricing power.
  • She should also check grants, since they neither dilute nor require repayment, but they are limited in source and must be a perfect fit.

Why this scores. Every bullet either names a course concept or ties one to a detail from the scenario. Notice how often the words "Maya", "bakery", "$60,000" and "20%" appear. An answer that defined monopolistic competition perfectly and never mentioned the bakery would lose most of the application marks.
