01Business

What a business actually is

An organisation is any group working toward a shared goal. A business is the subset that intends to make a profit. The intent is the entire distinction.

MEMORISE The 6 characteristics of a business 6
  1. Requires organisation and effort
  2. Attempts to provide the things people need or want
  3. Must try to satisfy customers' needs
  4. Generates revenue
  5. Incurs expenses
  6. Tries to make a profit
Hook

They are a sequence, not a list. You organise effort to provide what people need, you must satisfy customers, which earns revenue, against which you pay expenses, and what is left is profit. Say the sentence and the six fall out in order.

MEMORISE Profit motive: the two named positions 2
  1. Karl Marx · profit is BAD. Owners are capitalists, workers are labourers, and owners merely exploit labour for profit. Gave rise to communism: the state should own all business activity so nobody grows rich off another's labour. The Communist Manifesto, Das Kapital.
  2. Adam Smith · profit is GOOD. Profit is a fair reward for the risk owners take. Rational people only commit resources where there is reward. The surplus buys equipment and marketing, and owners create the jobs. Gave rise to liberalism: leave people to their self-interest with minimum government interference. The Wealth of Nations, founder of economics.
Numbers worth carrying into a case answer

Canada is the 2nd easiest country on earth to start a business, out of 190 studied by the World Bank. New Zealand is first. But roughly 140,000 businesses are created every year in Canada and about 120,000 close, which is 380 new businesses every day against a nearly equal number of failures. Amazon lost about $3 billion over eight years before its first profit. Easy to start, very hard to keep.

MEMORISE Why businesses fail 4

Running a business is:

  1. a human endeavour
  2. complicated
  3. difficult
  4. unpredictable, despite your best predictions

All of which adds up to one word: RISK.

MEMORISE The value package 3
  1. Function: what the product is intended to do
  2. Features: additional attributes giving improved usefulness or experience
  3. Benefits: the advantage derived from purchasing it
FUNCTION + FEATURES + BENEFITS = VALUE
MEMORISE The 3 types of consumer product 3
  1. Convenience: inexpensive, purchased frequently, little time or effort
  2. Shopping: moderately expensive, infrequent, consumers compare features, benefits and price
  3. Specialty: great importance attached, consumers spend much time and effort finding exactly what they want

Separately: B2C consumer products go to the end user, B2B industrial products are parts, ingredients, materials and supplies one business buys from another.

MEMORISE The 4 stages of the product life cycle 4
  1. Introduction: product or technology new and little known. Few buyers (early adopters, risk of the unknown), high price (low volume), high marketing spend to educate. 80% of new products fail here.
  2. Growth: demand expands rapidly. Buyers growing (trial, accessibility), price falling due to economies of scale. This is where companies start to make a profit.
  3. Maturity: sales peak. Buyers slowing due to saturation, marketing spend lower because awareness is already high. This is where profit is highest.
  4. Decline: demand falls, prices fall, profits fall, competitors decline. Usually caused by newer or better products.

Extension = any effort to re-package, re-launch or update a mature but well-known product.

03Factors

What a business is made of

Four building blocks combine to create any business. Three are inputs. The fourth is the one that assembles the other three.

MEMORISE The 4 factors of production 4
  1. Natural resources: things found in nature, grown out of or extracted from the earth. Land, water, wood, oil, wheat.
  2. Labour: the people who contribute their efforts to a business.
  3. Capital: money, or the machines and technologies money can buy.
  4. Entrepreneurship: the willingness to take initiative and accept the risk of failure in return for suitable reward.

Definition: the basic building blocks that, in combination, are required to create a business and produce goods and services.

MEMORISE The 3 revolutions 3

  2. Industrial Revolution: 18th and 19th centuries. A large number of labour-saving inventions, mostly mechanical, transformed the most important industries of the age. Capital became more important than labour. Work moved from homes into the factory system.

04Economies

Who owns the factors

An economic system is the means by which society produces and distributes what its people need and want. The answer runs on one axis from total state control to total private control.

MEMORISE An economic system determines 4 things 4
  1. Who owns the factors of production
  2. Who controls the factors of production
  3. Who decides what needs to be produced
  4. Who decides how goods and services are distributed
MEMORISE 2 families, 2 members each 4
  1. PLANNED → Communism: all factors controlled by the state, no private property. What Marx envisioned. No pure examples today, North Korea closest.
  2. PLANNED → Socialism: government owns or controls the majority of factors and directs most production. Individuals can own some private enterprise (restaurants, taxis, retail). Past: USSR, Eastern Europe. Today: China, Cuba, Vietnam.
  3. MARKET → Pure capitalism: all factors privately owned, no taxes, no regulation. Does not exist anywhere. Assumes social Darwinism.
  4. MARKET → Mixed market: ownership mostly private, but government provides a stable environment through public services, laws, regulation and oversight. Canada, USA, Japan, Germany. Most of the globe.
MEMORISE The 3 roles of government in a mixed market 3
  1. Impose and collect taxes
  2. Pass laws that regulate the conduct of businesses
  3. Provide some goods and services, occasionally in competition with private enterprise
MEMORISE The 4 types of tax 4
  1. Personal income tax: based on annual income, and it is progressive: takes a larger percentage from high earners than from low earners.
  2. Corporate income tax: businesses pay on their profits.
  3. Sales tax (HST): on goods and services bought. 13% in Ontario.
  4. Employment insurance: about 2% of annual income, funding the federal EI programme.

Why taxes: to pay for services, and to redistribute from those who can afford it to those in need.

MEMORISE The 5 named regulations 5
  1. Canadian Human Rights Act: requires all affected by the organisation be treated equally
  2. Canada Labour Code: sets minimum wages, limits hours per day
  3. Employment Insurance Act: obliges employers to contribute to EI, allows required maternity leave (12 to 18 months)
  4. Competition Act: prohibits misleading advertising and claims
  5. Consumer Packaging and Labelling Act: requires specific information on packaging
MEMORISE The 5 named crown corporations 5
  1. CBC, Canadian Broadcasting Corporation
  2. Canada Post
  3. LCBO, Liquor Control Board of Ontario
  4. Hydro One, electricity utility
  5. OLG, chain of casinos

Crown corporation = an enterprise owned and operated by the Government of Canada.

MEMORISE The historical terms, one per country 7
  1. Nationalization (USSR), government assumes ownership and control of resources, businesses or industries to benefit the nation
  2. New Deal (USA), 1930s laws and executive orders giving government power to manage the economy and provide employment
  3. State-owned enterprise (UK), government-owned organisation providing goods and services but not seeking profit
  4. Great Leap Forward (China), Mao's policy abolishing private land ownership, forcing state-owned communes
  5. Command economy: a synonym for planned economy implying more authoritarianism
  6. Perestroika: 1980s Soviet laws reversing collectivization and nationalization
  7. Privatization: transferring ownership out of government control into private hands
The arguments, for the case section

For planned: only the state has the power to feed, clothe, shelter, educate and employ a population, and only the state has a benevolent interest in everyone's welfare rather than profit. Against: planners work from models, models do not guarantee correct quantities, and bureaucracies are slow to adapt.

For market: needs are too vast for one entity to plan, officials are too removed and lack the information and motivation, competition keeps enterprise efficient, and concentrated power corrupts. Against: large private firms get bureaucratic and unresponsive too.

05Markets

How markets compete

A market is not a place, it is the interaction of buyers and sellers. The single variable defining a market structure is how many sellers there are, because that sets both buyer choice and seller pricing power.

MEMORISE The 4 market structures 4
  1. Perfect competition: a large number of small sellers, all offering more or less the same product at more or less the same price. Low or no barriers to entry, undifferentiated products, similar prices.
  2. Oligopoly: products available from only a small number (3 to 5) of large suppliers. Limited choice. High barriers to entry.
  3. Monopoly: only one supplier. 100% market share by definition, and can set whatever price it likes.
  4. Monopolistic competition: a combination of all three. Very many sellers with low barriers and small share, but a few are much larger, differentiated and branded, and can charge more. Coffee shops, athletic clothing, fast food.
MEMORISE The 2 types of monopoly 2
  1. Natural monopoly: wins by economics. For reasons of size, greater efficiency or exclusive access to resources or technologies it will always be cheaper than any rival. Examples: NFL, NHL, NBA.
  2. Legislated monopoly: wins by law. Enjoys the exclusive right to sell in a market because government granted it through legislation. Examples: Bell Canada, Rogers cable, LCBO.
MEMORISE The 4 barriers to entry 4
  1. Technological advantage: competitors developed processes lowering their costs
  2. Scale: capital intensive with high start-up costs, or labour intensive
  3. Size / economies of scale
  4. Government regulation: banks, for instance

Barriers to exit also exist: contractual obligations, large investment, specialised equipment.

The interactions of buyers and sellers exchanging information about products and services for sale. An abstract idea, not a physical place.

Also called the degree of competition. The various combinations of numbers of buyers and sellers in a market.

Market price

A market characterised by a large number of small sellers, all offering more or less the same product for more or less the same price, with lots of buyer choice.

06Measuring

Measuring the whole economy

Six measures, and each exists because the one before it was incomplete. Learn them as a chain of complaints and you cannot mix them up.

MEMORISE The 6 measures, with the one-word gloss for each 6
  1. GDP = SIZE of the economy. Total value of all goods and services produced within a country during a given period, usually one year.
  2. GDP growth = PROGRESS over time. The percentage change from one period to the next.
  3. GDP per capita = PROSPERITY of the average citizen. GDP divided by population.
  4. Productivity = THE CAUSE OF PROSPERITY. A ratio measuring how much gets achieved relative to the inputs used to achieve it.
  5. Employment and unemployment = how well the system is using its labour.
  6. Distribution and equality = how income is spread within the population.
Hook

Say it as a sentence: size, progress, prosperity, cause, labour, spread. Each measure answers the complaint about the one before it. GDP says nothing about direction, growth says nothing per person, per capita says nothing about why, productivity says nothing about who is working, and none of them say how evenly it is shared.

MEMORISE What causes high productivity 3
  1. Good labour: well educated, well trained, healthy workers
  2. Good natural resources: clean water, fertile soil
  3. Lots of capital: modern technology, good infrastructure, housing

These are just the factors of production again. A wealthy country has high productivity.

MEMORISE Business cycle vocabulary 4
  1. Business cycle: the expansion and contraction of a nation's economic activity over a period of years, periodically and with great regularity. Booms and busts.
  2. Recession: two consecutive quarters (periods of three months) when GDP declines.
  3. Depression: an unusually long or deep recession. USA 1929 to 1932.
  4. The Great Recession: worst in 100 years. Too many people borrowed too much to buy houses they could not afford. House values fell, loans went unpaid, banks collapsed, and a cycle of fear followed.
MEMORISE Employment: what counts and what does not 5
  1. Employment: having paid work.
  2. Labour force: people aged 15 and over who have a job or business, plus those without work who are actively seeking work.
  3. Unemployment rate: the percentage of the labour force actively seeking work but unable to find it.
  4. Under-employment: an employment situation where a person is not working at their full potential. NOT counted.
  5. Discouraged workers: people who withdrew from the labour force because they judged their chances of finding employment too remote to bother trying. NOT counted.
The trap in this unit

The unemployment rate counts only people actively seeking work, so the under-employed and discouraged workers are invisible in it. The headline number therefore understates the problem. If a case asks why unemployment looks fine but the economy does not, that is the answer.

MEMORISE Inequality: the 3 terms 3
  1. Lorenz curve: a diagram used to graphically represent the distribution of income or wealth within a population. Max Lorenz, 1905.
  2. Gini coefficient: a measure of inequality of income or wealth distribution, expressed as a decimal between 0 and 1. 0 = perfect equality, 1 = extreme inequality. Formula A / (A + B).
  3. Working poor: people who have employment but whose incomes are too low to save money or afford all the necessities of life.
Said outright in the lecture

You will not have to do the maths on the Gini coefficient. You do need to understand its relative meaning: closer to 0 is more equal, closer to 1 is less equal. Learn the direction, skip the arithmetic.

07Entrepreneurs

Who starts businesses

Entrepreneurship is a temperament and personality type, not a job description. It means to try, fail, learn from the failure, and try again.

MEMORISE The 3 psychological traits of entrepreneurs 3
  1. Need for achievement: a desire to achieve challenging goals
  2. Risk-taking propensity: a belief in one's own ability to influence affairs
  3. Tolerance of ambiguity: a willingness to tolerate some uncertainty

Entrepreneurship is an art. The three reinforce each other: high achievement need correlates with internal locus of control, which correlates with higher risk-taking propensity.

MEMORISE McClelland's 3 motivational needs 3
  1. Need for power (nPow): motivated to have authority and be influential. Choose careers offering status and prestige: senior managers at large corporations, senior military officers, politicians.
  2. Need for affiliation (nAff): need friendly relationships, motivated by interaction with others: teachers, doctors and nurses, religious leaders, coaches, roles involving teams.
  3. Need for achievement (nAch): motivated by a sense of personal achievement, like to set and attain challenging goals: athletes, intellectuals and scholars, entrepreneurs and business owners.

David McClelland, The Achieving Society, 1961. We are all driven by a mix of the three.

MEMORISE Achievement-motivated people: 4 further characteristics 4
  1. Sense of achievement is more important than financial or material reward
  2. Accomplishing the goal gives greater personal satisfaction than praise or recognition
  3. Financial reward is a measurement of success but not the end goal
  4. Neither security nor status are prime motivators
MEMORISE Risk: the 4 words that get confused 4
  1. Risk tolerance: a tendency to regard new or previously unknown situations as exciting and possibly desirable.
  2. Risk aversion: a tendency to regard them as threatening.
  3. Risk seeking: being careless of the consequences. Entrepreneurs are NOT this. They are not gamblers.
  4. Calculated risk: an enterprise undertaken after the advantages and disadvantages have been considered and the probability of various outcomes calculated.
MEMORISE The 3 inheritances 3
  1. Entrepreneurial inheritance: the characteristics and values acquired from exposure to an entrepreneurial parent
  2. Vocational inheritance: the tendency to observe and learn work-related or job-related skills and aptitudes from parents
  3. Economic inheritance: the tendency for children of entrepreneurs to receive financial capital to support their desire to start a business

About 80% of Canadian small business owners come from a family business background.

HookEVE

Entrepreneurial, Vocational, Economic. Values, then skills, then money. Most abstract to most concrete.

MEMORISE Why older entrepreneurs succeed: the 4 C's 4
  1. Capital
  2. Contacts
  3. Credibility
  4. Confidence

More than half of Canadian businesses are owned by people over 50, and the highest rate of new business creation belongs to the 55 to 64 age group.

MEMORISE Immigrant entrepreneurs: pulled and pushed 2
  1. PULLED, by psychological characteristics, self-confidence, willingness to take reasonable risks to achieve a desired goal, high need for achievement. These match the entrepreneurial profile.
  2. PUSHED, by economic circumstances, lacking contacts and social networks, lacking local Canadian experience or track record. Entrepreneurship becomes an option.

Economic migrant = someone who moves from one country to another in order to improve their economic wellbeing and that of their family. The majority of immigrants to Canada are economic migrants.

MEMORISE Business size thresholds 3
  1. Small: under 50 employees for services, under 100 for goods
  2. Medium: 100 to 499 employees
  3. Large: 500 or more employees

SME covers small and medium together. There is no official definition of a small business. Other thresholds used: under $5M revenue, under $1M exports, under $500K profits.

Free enterprise

MEMORISE Sole proprietor: the 5 "alls" 5

  3. Keeps all the business profits
  4. Pays all the business expenses and taxes
  5. Responsible for all the business debts and obligations

Key advantage SIMPLICITY, key disadvantage LIABILITY. One owner does not mean one employee. Most common form of ownership, and starting one can be free.

The named cases and discussions

MEMORISE Case Study 1: BlackBerry, the product life cycle in one company 4
  1. Introduction: a secure two-way pager and email device sold to business, not consumers. Little known, expensive, one narrow use.
  2. Growth: the physical keyboard plus locked-down corporate email made it the default business phone. Demand expands fast, competitors arrive.
  3. Maturity: sales peak around 2011. Everyone who wants one has one, and the touchscreen rivals now match it on the things buyers care about.
  4. Decline: the iPhone and Android reset what a phone is (touchscreen, app store, consumer first). BlackBerry defended the keyboard instead of extending. Demand, price, profit and finally the handset business all fall away, leaving software and licensing.
Hook

BlackBerry is the exam's favourite case because it is the whole life cycle inside one brand, and because it shows the specific failure the course cares about: a mature product with no extension. If a question asks why a business declined, "it did not re-package, re-launch or update a mature product" is the phrase that earns the mark.

MEMORISE Case Study 2: technology for playing music 7
  1. 1930s, 40s, 50s: 78 rpm records
  2. 1950s, 60s, mid 70s: 33 rpm records
  3. early to mid 70s: 8 track cassettes
  4. mid 70s to 1990s: 16 track cassettes
  5. 1985 to 2005: CDs
  6. 2000 onwards: downloads
  7. NEXT??? streaming, and whatever replaces it
Hook

Seven formats, one unchanged human want: listening to music. The need never entered a life cycle; every product that served it did, and each new one killed the last. That is the point of the case. It is also the answer to "can an industry be in decline while demand is rising?", yes, because the industry is the format, not the want.

MEMORISE Discussion: economic systems, what is the right balance? 3
  1. Canada: public healthcare. Closer to the planned end. Government funds and directs it.
  2. USA: mostly private healthcare. Closer to the market end. Individuals and firms decide.
  3. UK: semi-private healthcare. A public system with a private tier alongside it.
Hook

There is no correct answer and the slide says so: the decision is difficult. What is being marked is whether you can argue the trade-off in the course's own terms. Planned buys equity of access and cost control and pays in choice and wait times. Market buys choice, innovation and speed and pays in unequal access. Every real country is a mixed market economy sitting somewhere on that line, which is exactly why the example is three countries and not one.

MEMORISE Financial intermediaries: who stands between savers and businesses 5
  1. Chartered banks, the largest source of business loans
  2. Credit unions and caisses populaires, member owned
  3. Trust companies
  4. Insurance companies and pension funds, which lend out the premiums and contributions they hold
  5. Investment dealers, who sell a business's shares and bonds to investors
Hook

This is the one place the lecture pointed straight at the textbook instead of covering it, so it is worth the two minutes. The idea is simple: a business almost never gets capital directly from the people who saved it. An intermediary pools many small deposits and lends them out as fewer large amounts, which is why it can carry risk that no single saver would. It is the plumbing underneath debt and equity from investors, the third and fourth of the four core ways of financing.

A not-for-profit financial intermediary owned by its members, who are also its depositors and borrowers.

A financial intermediary that sells a business's shares or bonds to investors on its behalf.

Case 1, market structure and financing (model answer)

Maya runs a small bakery in a neighbourhood with four other bakeries. Hers is the only one with a recognisable logo, a name people repeat, and a signature sourdough people travel for. She charges 20% more than the others and still sells out. She wants to open three more locations and needs $400,000. She has $60,000 saved and no assets to secure a loan against.

a) Identify the market structure and justify it.

  • Monopolistic competition.
  • Many sellers with low barriers to entry, but a small number differentiate through branding and can charge more.
  • Applies here: five bakeries in one neighbourhood is many sellers with low barriers, but Maya has a recognisable logo and a signature product, which is differentiation and branding.
  • Evidence: she charges 20% more and still sells out, which is pricing power that perfect competition would not permit.

b) Recommend a financing route and state the trade-off.

  • $60,000 is founder equity, leaving $340,000 to raise.
  • Debt is difficult: no assets to secure against, and younger owners have less access to debt capital.
  • So investor equity, likely angel investors at this amount and stage.
  • Trade-off: she gains capital plus the investor's skills and expertise, but accepts dilution and loss of decision-making power, so less control over the brand that is currently the source of her pricing power.
  • She should also check grants, which neither dilute nor require repayment, but are limited in source and must be a perfect fit.

Why this scores. Every bullet either names a course concept or ties one to a detail from the scenario. Notice how often "Maya", "bakery", "$60,000" and "20%" appear.

Case 2, product life cycle and structure (model answer)

Dev launched a fitness tracker two years ago. Sales climbed steeply for eighteen months, then flattened. Almost everyone in his target market who wants one now owns one. He has stopped advertising heavily because everyone already knows the brand, and this is his most profitable year yet. He runs it as a sole proprietorship. A customer is threatening to sue over a faulty battery.

a) Identify the life cycle stage with evidence.

  • Maturity.
  • Sales peak, buyers slow due to market saturation, marketing spend falls because awareness is already high, and profit is at its highest.
  • All four match Dev: sales flattened, "almost everyone who wants one owns one" is saturation, he stopped advertising because the brand is known, and it is his most profitable year.
  • Next stage is decline, usually triggered by newer or better products. He should consider an extension, repackaging or updating the tracker.

b) Advise on his legal structure given the lawsuit.

  • As a sole proprietor Dev has unlimited personal liability: no limit on what he might pay, and it is not capped at his investment in the business.
  • His personal assets are exposed to this battery claim.
  • He should incorporate. The key advantage of a corporation is limited liability: a shareholder's responsibility is limited to the capital they provided.
  • Costs of doing so: complexity, cost, legal requirements for directors and officers, and paying tax twice.
  • General rule: if a business has any real chance of risk or danger, it should not be organised as a sole proprietorship.
Case 3, factors, economic system and measurement (model answer)

A country has large oil reserves, a small and poorly educated workforce, and little modern machinery. The government owns the oil industry, the banks and the railways, but people may run restaurants and shops. GDP grew 4% last year while population grew 6%. The richest 20% receive 61% of income.

a) Classify the economic system.

  • Socialism, which is a planned economy.
  • Government owns or controls the majority of the factors of production and directs most production, but individuals may own some private enterprise.
  • Applies here: the state owns oil, banks and railways, which are the large important industries, while restaurants and shops stay private. That is the socialist pattern exactly, not communism, which would permit no private property at all.

b) Comment on factor intensity and productivity.

  • Resource intensive: the economy depends heavily on natural resources, its oil.
  • Productivity is the ratio of output to input, and its three causes are good labour, good natural resources and plenty of capital.
  • This country has one of the three. The workforce is small and poorly educated, and there is little modern machinery, so labour and capital are both weak.
  • Therefore productivity, and so prosperity, will be low despite the resource wealth.

c) Interpret the two statistics.

  • GDP grew 4% but population grew 6%, so GDP per capita fell. The economy is bigger but the average citizen is worse off. This is exactly why per capita exists as a separate measure from growth.
  • The richest 20% receiving 61% of income indicates high inequality, which would show as a Lorenz curve bowed far from the line of perfect equality and a Gini coefficient closer to 1.
  • Note that a recession has not occurred: that requires two consecutive quarters of declining GDP, and GDP grew.
